25:Tf70,<h2 id="overview">Overview</h2>
<p>The Grant Lab Research Platform is the operational layer behind a large part of the microscopy processing work in the Seth Grant Lab. It is not a single tool. It is a research software platform that brings together services, data workflows, internal interfaces, and compute orchestration so researchers can move from raw microscopy data to analysis-ready outputs.</p>
<p>This is one of the strongest examples of my systems range because it combines product engineering, infrastructure, workflow design, and ongoing operations in one environment.</p>
<h2 id="problem">Problem</h2>
<p>Research labs often accumulate scripts, desktop tools, and disconnected processing steps over time. That works until scale, reproducibility, collaboration, and maintenance start to matter more than one-off execution.</p>
<p>The problem here was to create a platform that could:</p>
<ul>
<li>coordinate long-running and heterogeneous microscopy workflows</li>
<li>support multiple internal tools without every new feature becoming operational chaos</li>
<li>use distributed and GPU-enabled processing where needed</li>
<li>remain maintainable for real researchers using it day to day</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="what-i-built">What I Built</h2>
<p>I built and evolved the platform as both an application layer and an operational system. That included:</p>
<ul>
<li>A Flask-based application layer with multiple route blueprints and internal tool surfaces</li>
<li>Distributed task execution using Celery and Dask</li>
<li>MySQL, Redis, and queue-backed background processing</li>
<li>Docker Swarm orchestration for multi-service deployment</li>
<li>Integration points for GPU-enabled and domain-specific processing tools</li>
<li>Operational patterns for testing, deployment, migrations, and service management</li>
</ul>
<p>The platform also became the place where related tools could live together coherently: ROI workflows, converters, segmentation, detection pipelines, heatmap generation, and supporting utilities.</p>
<h2 id="architecture-and-technical-decisions">Architecture and Technical Decisions</h2>
<h3>Service orchestration</h3>
<p>The platform runs as an orchestrated set of services, including:</p>
<ul>
<li>web application layer</li>
<li>Celery workers</li>
<li>Dask scheduler and workers</li>
<li>MySQL</li>
<li>Redis</li>
<li>reverse proxy and deployment edge services</li>
<li>supporting internal tools such as ROI interfaces</li>
</ul>
<h3>Workflow design</h3>
<p>The key architectural decision was to treat the lab's processing environment as a platform rather than as a set of separate scripts. That made it possible to standardise:</p>
<ul>
<li>how jobs are submitted and tracked</li>
<li>how results are stored and handed off</li>
<li>how new tools are added</li>
<li>how compute-heavy steps are moved onto the right execution layer</li>
</ul>
<h3>Operational quality</h3>
<p>This is not just a prototype stack. It needed to be stable enough for repeated use and flexible enough for new research workflows. Testing, deployment, migrations, and maintainability mattered as much as feature delivery.</p>
<h2 id="constraints">Constraints</h2>
<p>The platform had to absorb a difficult mix of requirements:</p>
<ul>
<li>long-running microscopy jobs</li>
<li>heterogeneous scientific tools and dependencies</li>
<li>internal users with very practical workflow needs</li>
<li>infrastructure that had to stay maintainable in a real research environment</li>
</ul>
<p>It also had to support scale: large datasets, many processing stages, and multiple tools sharing a common operational base.</p>
<h2 id="outcome">Outcome</h2>
<p>The Grant Lab Research Platform became the backbone for microscopy processing and related research tooling in the lab.
